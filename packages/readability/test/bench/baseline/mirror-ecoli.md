# Mum's fight for answers over son's death from E.coli after swimming in Blackpool sea

## EXCLUSIVE: A mum who was told there was "no way" E.coli could survive in the water reveals the exact moment she discovered the truth, three decades on from the death of her son

07:47, 06 Sep 2026

A mother who believes her son’s death nearly 30 years ago may have been caused by contact with [sewage](https://www.mirror.co.uk/all-about/sewage) at the beach has spoken of her fight to obtain his medical records.

Article continues below

Rachael Bell said she always blamed herself for the death of beloved son Matthew, at the age of just under four, in 1997. The youngster initially suffered from diarrhoea and agonising stomach cramps, and soon become seriously ill.

‌  

It was diagnosed as [E. coli](https://www.mirror.co.uk/all-about/ecoli) 0157, the most dangerous strain of the pathogen, and Matthew went on to develop haemolytic uraemic syndrome, a rare condition that can lead to kidney failure.

‌  

Matthew’s younger brother, Tom, also contracted the same illness, but thankfully battled through and recovered. However, Matthew’s condition worsened and he was transferred to Liverpool’s Alder Hey Children’s Hospital where, after a traumatic few weeks, he went into a coma and was moved to intensive care but tragically died towards the end of October.

Rachael, 61, who lives in the village of Torrisholme, on the outskirts of Morecambe, said the unbelievable sadness was compounded by questions from a local authority environmental health officer who was sent to investigate the source of the E.coli.

‌  

He had list of questions about what the boys had eaten, where Rachael had shopped, and how she prepared food. “He was abrupt,” added Rachael, who used to work as a cook and a housekeeper. “He was saying ‘where do you keep your meat?’”

Yet Rachael said she also raised the fact the boys had been to the beach at nearly Blackpool not long before they became ill, and had played in the sea and on the sand.

“He said ‘no way it’ll survive is water’,” recalls Rachael. However, that turned out not to be case, with evidence that E.coli can, in fact, survive for up to 91 days in water.

Still, Rachael was oblivious to this, and spent the decades since battling with the thought that Matthew’s death was somehow her fault. “I have lived all my life thinking it was me to blame,” she said.

All that changed when sat down earlier this year to watch [Channel 4’s hard-hitting factual drama 'Dirty Business'](https://www.mirror.co.uk/all-about/dirty-business), about the fight by campaigners to expose the scandal of raw sewage dumping by water firms.

‌  

It also featured the true story of Heather Preen, the eight-year-old girl who died after contracting the same strain of E.coli as Matthew while on a beach holiday with her family in 1999. Heather’s mum, Julie Maughan, believed Heather’s exposure to sewage from a storm outflow may have been the source, but criticised officials for their slow response.

Watching the programme proved life-changing for Rachael, as she thought back to her own suspicions at the time of Matthew’s death.

In a bid to find out more, Rachael first contacted Alder Hey Hospital in March this year to get a copy of Matthew’s medical records. Despite filling in forms four times, making phone calls and sending emails since, she was still left waiting. “I want justice for him, I think he deserves that,” Rachael says. “The long wait has compounded the agony. It made it feel like his life wasn’t important.”

Alder Hey said in a statement: “We don’t comment on individual cases.” However, after being contacted by the [Mirror](https://www.mirror.co.uk/), and following six months of waiting, Matthew’s medical records have finally been sent. Rachael said last night she would now begin going through the documents, that run to more than 500 pages.
